Overview

Real vs. Hollywood Science. What "2012" Gets Wrong.

The movie “2012”, directed by Roland Emmerich, follows a global apocalypse encapsulating both personal survival and large scale political crises. The movie centers on Jackson Curtis (John Cusack) a writer and divorced father, as he tries to protect his family as the government secretly prepares for a worldwide catastrophe. The film also follows scientists and international leaders as they discover that Earth is becoming unstable and that massive disasters are about to destroy cities, coastlines, and populations world wide. The movie mixes family drama, political secrecy, and action packed destruction scenes that allows “2012” to remain a memorable film nearly 17 years later. Emmerich’s “2012” evokes a true sense of peril at the hands of a natural disaster, resulting in an encapsulating and intense film. The “science” used in the film, however, is often highly exaggerated or imaginative. The film includes massive earthquakes, collapsing crust throughout a city, megatsunamis, and sudden world wide storms, presented as part of one chain reaction. While the movie attributes the earthquakes to neutrinos from a massive solar flare causing the earth’s core to overheat, in reality, earthquakes occur when two plates can no longer withstand the friction amongst themselves and “two blocks of earth suddenly slip past one another.” (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.)

Similarly, the tsunami scenes are dramatized. Tsunamis are generated when an earthquake along a subduction zone occurs and the plate edge pushing into the subducting plate “breaks free and springs seaward, raising the sea floor and the water above it” (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, n.d.). This rise causes a tsunami. This differs from the movies depiction of global flooding triggered by a planet-wide crustal collapse. This movie review looks to examine how “2012” turns real geologic and atmospheric concepts into captivating, yet scientifically inaccurate, scenes. The contrast is what makes the film both entertaining and worth scientifically analyzing.

Earthquakes

Earthquake Inaccuracies

In “2012”, the earthquake scene begins as Jackson Curtis and his family try to escape Los Angeles while the ground begins to violently shake beneath them. As they drive through the city, roads split apart, buildings collapse, and entire sections of Los Angeles appear to fall into a widening crater in the ground. The destruction worsens quickly. Highways tear open, skyscrapers tilt and crash, and the city is portrayed as being swallowed by an enormous chasm. The scene suggests that there has been an earthquake on the San Andreas fault so forceful that it rips the city open and causes the ground to collapse into the earth. While this creates a cinematic disaster sequence, it is not how the San Andreas Fault actually behaves.

The San Andreas fault moves horizontally. One example provided by USGS says, “If a person stood on one side of the fault and looked across it, the block on the opposite side would appear to have moved right” (Schulz & Wallace, 2016). Scientists describe the movement of the San Andreas fault as “right-lateral strike-slip.” This means the two sides should move horizontally past each other, not a vertical collapse depicted in this scene. Although the San Andreas Fault can produce major earthquakes, “the simple truth is that there are no known faults capable of generating a magnitude 10 or larger ‘mega-quake’” (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.). The earthquake in “2012” is said to be a magnitude 10.9. While there have been large earthquakes recorded (the 1906 and 1857 earthquakes, both estimated to have a magnitude of 7.9), the San Andreas fault is simply not long or deep enough to generate a 9+ magnitude earthquake. Regardless, with even the most violent earthquakes, massive chasms depicted in the movie are not scientifically accurate.

Tsunamis

Earthquake-generated tsunamis and the movie’s impossible flood scenario.

One of the primary adrenaline inducing scenes in “2012” is the megatsunami that crests over the Himalayan mountain range. In one of the film’s most climactic moments, the survivors scramble to board the arks hidden in the mountains of Tibet, when a colossal wave (said to be thousands of feet high) rolls across Asia and washes over the peaks of the Himalayas. A buddhist monk watching from a clifftop witnesses the wave consume everything below before it continues to engulf him. The film frames this tsunami as the culmination of its chain reaction catastrophe: the crustal shifts have displaced so much ocean water that the planet is effectively submerged. While it is one of the most iconic moments in the film, it is the most scientifically implausible as well. In real life, a tsunami occurs when an earthquake on a subduction zone has a “leading, edge of the overriding plate that breaks free and springs seaward, raising the sea floor and the water above it” (NOAA, n.d.). The earthquake must occur at a location with a shallow seafloor that displaces the seafloor. Thrust earthquakes are far more likely to generate tsunamis than strike-slip earthquakes, which is what occurs on the San Andreas fault. The disaster in “2012” is driven not by a subduction zone rupture beneath the ocean, but by a broad crustal displacement across continents. In the movie there is no shown offshore seafloor uplift, no subduction event, and no ocean column displacement of the kind that real tsunamis require.

Setting aside the cause, the scale of the wave itself defies physics. The maximum height a tsunami reaches is referred to as the “run up.” Runups are typically ten feet high, but can exceed 100 feet (NOAA, 2023). Because the Himalayas are nearly 30,000 feet above sea level, the movie implies there is ~30,000+ feet of runup. This is tens of thousands of feet above anything recorded. Additionally, towards the end of the film, the arks in Tibet and the Himalayas are inundated. Large tsunamis have been proven to flood low-lying coastal areas more than a mile inland, but the Himalayas sit hundreds of miles from the nearest coastline. This is a distance the largest of tsunamis would not be able to sustain the heights depicted in the film. The film certainly highlights real catastrophes, displacement of ocean water following seismic activity, but scales it to a magnitude that is unrealistic.

Storms

Sudden worldwide storms and the limits of real atmospheric science.

As the disasters in the film escalate, “2012” presents the world as deteriorating not only geologically but meteorologically as well. Following the LA earthquake and the eruption at Yellowstone, the movie depicts a planet in full atmospheric collapse. Massive storms pound the coastlines, the weather across the globe shifts violently and without warning, and the combination of seismic and volcanic activity is framed as the trigger for the global environmental breakdown. The film’s chain reaction logic suggests that the overheating of the Earth’s core sets off the earthquakes, which feed into the Yellowstone eruption, whose ash cloud rapidly engulfs the continent, which contributes to the worldwide chaos, all occurring within a few days. These storms are all presented as one continuous interlocked catastrophe. While volcanic eruptions can have large-scale atmospheric consequences, the film dramatically compresses the timeline and exaggerates the mechanism. In reality, a volcano’s effect on global weather unfolds over years, not hours or days. According to the USGS, during major eruptions, massive amounts of “volcanic gas, aerosol droplets, and ash are injected into the stratosphere" (USGS, n.d.). The sulfur dioxide then converts to sulfuric acid forming sulfate aerosols that reflects solar radiation back into space, causing the Earth’s temperature to decrease. One example of this cooling is the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines, the largest eruption of the twentieth century. Following the eruption, the earth cooled for three years, bringing the temperature of the Earth down by 1.3 degrees Fahrenheit. This slow, gradual cooling further demonstrates that there would not be the instant global reaction as depicted in the film. NASA further reinforces how modest the effects of volcanic eruptions are on a global scale, writing, “[researchers] found that post-eruption cooling would probably not exceed 2.7 degrees Fahrenheit for even the most powerful blasts” (Goddard Digital Team, 2024). Thus, while atmospheric events may occur as a result of an eruption, the movie severely shortens the timeline and magnitude at which this would happen.

The film also implies a direct causal link between the seismic activity and instantaneous global weather shifts. The sequence of events allows for viewers to believe that earthquakes themselves rearrange atmospheric systems instantly. According to USGS, there exists a correlation between earthquakes and weather, only such that hurricanes can “occasionally trigger what are known as ‘slow earthquakes’” (Kacenelenbogen, 2022). However, the data behind this correlation are not statistically significant. This proves that the evidence we do have shows that the relationship between weather events and earthquakes is opposite to what is portrayed in the movie (if there is a relationship at all).”2012” portrays “real” concepts like volcanic winter and atmospheric disruption, but inaccurately groups the events into a single, instantaneous chain reaction.
