Overview

Real vs. Hollywood Science. What "2012" Gets Wrong.

The movie “2012”, directed by Roland Emmerich, follows a global apocalypse encapsulating both personal survival and large scale political crises. The movie centers on Jackson Curtis (John Cusack) a writer and divorced father, as he tries to protect his family as the government secretly prepares for a worldwide catastrophe. The film also follows scientists and international leaders as they discover that Earth is becoming unstable and that massive disasters are about to destroy cities, coastlines, and populations world wide. The movie mixes family drama, political secrecy, and action packed destruction scenes that allows “2012” to remain a memorable film nearly 17 years later. Emmerich’s “2012” evokes a true sense of peril at the hands of a natural disaster, resulting in an encapsulating and intense film. The “science” used in the film, however, is often highly exaggerated or imaginative. The film includes massive earthquakes, collapsing crust throughout a city, megatsunamis, and sudden world wide storms, presented as part of one chain reaction. While the movie attributes the earthquakes to neutrinos from a massive solar flare causing the earth’s core to overheat, in reality, earthquakes occur when two plates can no longer withstand the friction amongst themselves and “two blocks of earth suddenly slip past one another.” (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.)

Similarly, the tsunami scenes are dramatized. Tsunamis are generated when an earthquake along a subduction zone occurs and the plate edge pushing into the subducting plate “breaks free and springs seaward, raising the sea floor and the water above it” (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, n.d.). This rise causes a tsunami. This differs from the movies depiction of global flooding triggered by a planet-wide crustal collapse. This movie review looks to examine how “2012” turns real geologic and atmospheric concepts into captivating, yet scientifically inaccurate, scenes. The contrast is what makes the film both entertaining and worth scientifically analyzing.

Earthquakes

Earthquake Inaccuracies

In the film, the city of Los Angeles is seen splitting apart and collapsing into a large chasm, implying that there has been an earthquake on the San Andreas Fault large enough to swallow entire streets and buildings. While the scenes in which the city is crumbling into the ground are cinematic, it is not scientifically accurate. The San Andreas fault is a transform, right lateral strike-slip fault. On this fault, movements are typically horizontal shearing not movements that cause the ground to pull apart vertically. Earthquakes occur when stress builds up along a fault until it exceeds the fault's frictional resistance, causing a sudden slip. This phenomenon, called elastic rebound, releases stored energy as seismic waves, producing shaking and fault displacement, not a city sized hole.

This film also exaggerates the amount of slip. Based on the scene, the rupture appears to extend across most of LA. This would mean fault lengths of tens to hundreds of kilometeres. However, the displacement shown, roads and buildings appearing to be seperated by tens to hundreds of meters, is far beyond what the San Andreas fault could produce. The largest earthquakes on this fault have only been shown to generate several meters of horizontal offset. For example, the 1906 San Francisco earthquake produced about 6 meters of right lateral displacement. While still substantial, this did not create a gaping hole in the city and is still nowhere near what is depicted in the movie. An earthquake size is measured by seismic moment, Mo=μAd, where μ is rigidity, A is rupture area, and d is displacement. If we were to estimate the size of earthquake needed to create a fault that large, the movie implies a rupture dimension and slip that requires an unfeasibly large earthquake. Even in the event of a historically large earthquake, the actual effects would be violent shaking, surface rupture, and structural collapse; not all of LA collapsing into the Earth. While the movie depicts an anxiety inducing catastrophe, San Andreas earthquakes are dangerous because of intense ground motion and lateral fault movement, not because they create enormous gaping holes.

Tsunamis

Earthquake-generated tsunamis and the movie’s impossible flood scenario.

Another major disaster in 2012 is the enormous tsunami that appears to overwhelm vast regions and ultimately reach the Himalayas. While earthquakes can generate tsunamis, they do so through sudden displacement of the seafloor, not simply because the whole planet begins breaking apart in every direction at once.

Storms

Sudden worldwide storms and the limits of real atmospheric science.

The film also shows abrupt global weather shifts that trigger massive storms and contribute to planetary instability. This idea builds suspense, but it compresses atmospheric processes into a sudden, near-instant global collapse that does not reflect how weather systems actually work.

Here, you can explain the difference between short-term weather, long-term climate patterns, and geologic hazards. You can argue that while extreme storms are real, the movie combines multiple disaster types into one chain reaction in a way that is not supported by accepted Earth science.
